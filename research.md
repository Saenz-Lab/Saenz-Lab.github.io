Every cell is bounded by a membrane whose molecular composition determines its physical properties — permeability, fluidity, mechanical stability. These properties are not fixed: they emerge from hundreds of lipid species assembled under genomic control and remodelled in response to the environment. Our lab studies how membranes are built, regulated, and adapted, combining membrane biophysics, lipidomics, and synthetic genomics to connect molecular composition to cellular function. We aim to decode the principles that make a living membrane work — and to use those principles to engineer cells with programmable physical behaviours.

## RNA–lipid interactions
*How do lipid membranes organize and regulate RNA?*
{: .research-question}

RNA can catalyse reactions, store information, and regulate gene expression — capabilities that make it central to both modern biology and scenarios for the origin of life. A key missing ingredient for RNA-based systems, whether prebiotic or synthetic, is a mechanism to organize RNA in space and coordinate its activity over time. We discovered that lipid membranes can fill this role: specific membrane compositions modulate ribozyme catalysis and RNA folding through direct, sequence-dependent interactions. These findings suggest that lipid membranes could have served as organizational platforms for early RNA-based life, providing a simple answer to how primordial RNA molecules could be regulated and compartmentalized without proteins. Building on this work, we are developing RNA-based sensors of membrane physical state, engineering lipid-sensitive riboswitches, and using *in vitro* selection (SELEX) to identify RNA aptamers that bind defined lipid compositions. This programme connects the origin of life to synthetic biology and therapeutic delivery: the same molecular principles that may have organized a prebiotic RNA–lipid world now guide the design of RNA-based tools for sensing, regulation, and medicine.

## Minimal cells and the role of lipidome complexity
*What is the minimal molecular basis for a living membrane, and why are lipidomes so complex?*
{: .research-question}

Cellular membranes contain tens to hundreds of lipid species, yet the functional significance of this complexity remains poorly understood. To address this question, we use the genomically minimal bacterium JCVI-Syn3B and the pathogen *Mycoplasma mycoides* as living model membrane systems in which lipidome composition can be experimentally controlled. By delivering chemically defined lipid diets, we have shown that as few as two lipid species can sustain a living cell — establishing the simplest known membrane capable of supporting life. However, minimization comes with clear costs: reduced growth, impaired mechanical robustness, and diminished capacity for homeoviscous adaptation. By systematically reintroducing lipidome complexity, we are mapping how lipid diversity determines membrane physical properties, metabolic efficiency, and cellular fitness. These experiments reveal that the lipidome is not merely a structural scaffold but an active determinant of how cells grow, divide, and respond to their environment.

## Encoding and programming membrane phenotypes
*How are membrane physical properties genomically encoded, and can we program them?*
{: .research-question}

The physical state of a membrane — its fluidity, thickness, permeability, and mechanical stability — is ultimately determined by genes that control lipid biosynthesis, remodelling, and transport. Yet the mapping from genotype to membrane phenotype remains largely uncharted. We are closing this gap through adaptive evolution experiments, single-cell phenotyping by FACS with biophysical probes, and whole-genome sequencing to identify the genomic determinants of membrane properties. In parallel, we are engineering membrane sense-and-response circuits into minimal cells: introducing two-component signalling systems that detect membrane physical state and coupling them to lipid biosynthetic pathways or fluorescent reporters. Looking ahead, lipid-sensitive riboswitches will provide an RNA-based route to membrane homeostasis, linking our work on RNA–lipid interactions to genome engineering. Together, these efforts aim to make membrane phenotype a programmable trait — enabling the design of synthetic cells with tailored physical behaviours.
