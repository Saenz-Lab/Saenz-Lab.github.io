<section class="hero">
  <h1>Engineering membrane phenotypes in minimal and synthetic cells</h1>
  <p>
    The Saenz Lab studies how lipid membranes give rise to physical phenotypes that shape
    cellular function. We combine lipid biochemistry, membrane biophysics, and synthetic
    genomics to understand how membrane phenotypes are encoded, regulated, and
    experimentally engineered.
  </p>
</section>

<section class="grid">

  <div class="card third">
    <h2>Membrane phenotypes</h2>
    <p class="meta">
      Cellular membranes exhibit measurable physical properties such as order, permeability,
      and stability. We study how these properties emerge from lipid composition and
      organization, and how they constrain cellular function.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="card third">
    <h2>Lipidomes as control spaces</h2>
    <p class="meta">
      Lipidomes are not static inventories but adaptable, genetically encoded design spaces.
      We investigate how lipid composition can be tuned to modulate membrane physical state
      and functional robustness.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="card third">
    <h2>Minimal and synthetic cells</h2>
    <p class="meta">
      Genomically minimal cells provide reduced experimental systems in which membrane
      complexity can be systematically removed and rebuilt, revealing design principles
      that are obscured in more complex organisms.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="card third">
    <h2>Regulation and homeostasis</h2>
    <p class="meta">
      We explore how cells sense and regulate membrane physical state, and how membrane
      phenotypes are coupled to lipid synthesis, metabolism, and gene regulation.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="card third">
    <h2>RNA–lipid interactions</h2>
    <p class="meta">
      RNA can interact directly with lipid membranes. We study how membrane composition and
      physical state influence RNA structure, activity, and stability, opening new avenues
      for membrane-sensitive regulation.
    </p>
  </div>

</section>

<section class="grid">
  <div class="card">
    <h2>Why membranes?</h2>
    <p>
      Membranes are not passive boundaries. Their physical properties shape transport,
      signaling, and energy transduction, and impose constraints on cellular organization.
      Understanding how membrane phenotypes arise — and how they can be engineered — is
      central to both cell biology and synthetic biology.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>
